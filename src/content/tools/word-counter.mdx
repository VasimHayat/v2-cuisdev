faq:
  - q: How does the tool decide what counts as a word?
    a: "Words are found with the browser's built-in Unicode word segmentation, the same rules used for double-click selection. Punctuation, symbols and emoji are not words, numbers are, and contractions or hyphenated compounds such as don't and well-known count as one word. Languages written without spaces, such as Chinese and Japanese, are split into dictionary words rather than counted as one long word."
  - q: Why does the character count differ from other tools?
    a: "Many counters count UTF-16 code units, so an emoji or a flag counts as two or more characters. This tool counts what a reader sees as one character, so a family emoji or an accented letter made of two code points counts once. The byte size shown under the character tile is the UTF-8 size, which is what some databases and APIs limit."
  - q: How is reading time calculated?
    a: "Reading time divides the word count by 238 words per minute, the average silent reading speed for adults reading non-fiction in English, measured in a 2019 meta-analysis of 190 studies. Speaking time uses 150 words per minute, a comfortable pace for presentations and podcasts. Both are estimates; technical text reads more slowly."
  - q: What is keyword density and what is a good value?
    a: "Keyword density is how often a word or phrase appears as a share of all words. It shows what a text is really about and catches accidental repetition. Search engines no longer reward a particular percentage, and repeating a phrase unnaturally can hurt, so use the table to check that your main topic is present and that no filler word dominates."
  - q: Are the length targets exact?
    a: "They count characters as described above. X counts most characters as one but some, such as CJK characters, as two, and shortens every link to 23 characters, so treat the X target as a guide. Search engines truncate titles and meta descriptions by pixel width rather than characters, so 60 and 155 to 160 characters are reliable rules of thumb, not hard limits."

## What the counts tell you

A word count is the first constraint on most writing: an essay with a limit, a product description that has to fit a card, a talk with a time slot. This counter shows every figure that usually matters at once, so you can see the effect of an edit as you make it instead of pasting text into several tools.

Words are counted the way a careful reader would count them. Contractions and hyphenated compounds count once, numbers count, punctuation and emoji do not, and languages written without spaces are segmented into real words. Characters are counted as the reader sees them, so an emoji or an accented letter is one character even when it is stored as several.

## Reading time, speaking time and targets

Reading time assumes 238 words per minute and speaking time 150, the commonly cited averages for adult readers and for presenters. The toolbar shows how much room is left against four everyday targets: an X post, a meta description, an SEO title and an SMS. A target turns red when the text is too long and amber when a meta description is shorter than the useful range.

## Keyword density

The density table lists the most frequent words or two-word phrases with their share of the text. Skipping stop words removes filler such as the, and and of, so the list shows what the text is actually about. Use it to confirm that the main topic appears, and to spot a word you have leaned on too often.
